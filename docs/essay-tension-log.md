## T6: "UX doesn't matter" vs defaults
- **Status:** open
- **Stop:** 1A
- **Raised in:** "What is software now?" (Movement 1), 2026-11-07. Drafting halted after "The screen moves to the far end of the wire.", before the §4 claim that users stop caring about vendor UX or brand.
- **Steelman A:** *UX fades; value moves to data plus capabilities.* A bespoke interface created its own lock-in: a proprietary grammar of screens and flows that users had to learn, and that made leaving expensive. Chat has no such grammar to learn. It runs on reading and writing, and Karpathy's point is that "these prompts are written in English" (B2), so the skill that operates one front end operates every other. Once rendering happens in the harness, the vendor's opinion of how the work should go is no longer the product; what is left to compete on is whether the store holds the data the user wants, in the structure they want, reachable the way they want (thesis §4). Open protocols (API, MCP, A2A) mean a harness default does not bind the user to any one data vendor, because every capability is callable from every harness. Even the strongest recent finding on defaults concedes the limit: "defaults are less effective when the alternative has strong brand recognition and product quality" (N2, p.212). If quality now lives in the data and capabilities, defaults lose their grip where it matters. And the user Karpathy describes is "orchestrating agents who do and acting as oversight" (B6): someone judging outcomes, not someone living inside a vendor's chrome.
- **Steelman B:** *Defaults win; the harness vendor captures the value.* People accept defaults, by large margins, even on choices they say they care about. Johnson and Goldstein found effective consent to organ donation of 4.25% to 27.5% across explicit-consent (opt-in) countries against 85.9% to 99.98% across presumed-consent (opt-out) countries, and donation rates "about twice as high when opting-out as when opting-in" in an online experiment where changing the answer took one click (N1, p.1338). Their three mechanisms (a default read as a recommendation, the effort of deciding, loss aversion) apply to any harness's configuration. In software the effect is priced: the court in the US search case accepted that "defaults have a powerful impact on consumer decisions" (N2, p.30) and found that "All of this makes the defaults extremely valuable", with $26.3 billion paid in 2021 in revenue share under the contracts that bought those placements (N2, p.105; p.232). So UX does not vanish when it leaves the data vendor. It moves to whoever owns the harness, which sets the default sources, tools and presentation, and the brand relationship and the value go with it (thesis tension 6). Users stop caring about the data vendor's UX because they have already accepted the harness vendor's. A historical-materialist reading is available to this side: control of the means of interaction. Cautions: N1 is organ donation, so its transfer to software is by analogy; N2 is a court's findings, not the companies' own words (D-17).
