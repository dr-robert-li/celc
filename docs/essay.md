## What is software now?

What is software once agents produce and operate it?

I mean the question literally. In November 2017 Andrej Karpathy argued that neural networks "represent the beginning of a fundamental shift in how we develop software", and drew the line in two sentences: "Software 1.0 is code we write. Software 2.0 is code written by the optimization based on an evaluation criterion" (Karpathy, 2017). In June 2025 he went a step further, giving the large language model "a new designation of Software 3.0" on the grounds that "your prompts are now programs that program the LLM. And remarkably, these prompts are written in English" (Karpathy, 2025a). By February 2026 he was describing a working day in which "you are not writing the code directly 99% of the time, you are orchestrating agents who do and acting as oversight" (Karpathy, 2026). Three statements from one practitioner, spread across a little over eight years, each further from the idea that software is something a person types. The question is no longer hypothetical.

The received view in our industry is that none of this changes what software is, only how quickly it can be made: the same lifecycle, with its slow parts compressed. I will come to who holds that view, and in what words, further on. I think it is wrong. This essay argues that software itself is being redefined, not merely sped up, and that its lifecycle has to change with it.

Before I go on, I should say where I stand, because it shapes what I look for. I am an actor-network theory practitioner by doctorate (Li, 2020), a historical materialist (Li, 2011), and now a CTO, which means I spend my working days on the question above with a budget attached. I will use each lens where it explains best, and say so when they pull against each other. After this paragraph the argument has to carry itself.

I will start with the thing itself, before its lifecycle: first what is being stripped out of software, then what is left.

For as long as most of us have built it, software has carried two things at once. One is the what: the data, rules and operations a business needs. The other is the how: the particular sequence of screens, fields, menus and confirmations through which a person was expected to get at them. The two were shipped together because they had to be, since nobody could reach the what except through somebody's how. That coupling is coming apart, and the how is the half being stripped away.

It helps to be blunt about what the how always was. A user interface is an opinion: a vendor's judgement about the lowest common denominator of how its users would want to work, fixed in layout and shipped identically to everyone. Some of those opinions were very good. They were still opinions.

Chat is a different kind of interface, because what it asks of the user is something they already have. It is built on reading and writing, skills that are learned but learned universally, so language is a standard that already exists at a scale no interface designer could build. That is the force of Karpathy's "remarkably" (Karpathy, 2025a): the programs are now written in the medium their users already read and write. Gesture or eye contact might be more intuitive still, but they carry too much individually particularised meaning for a model's averaging to capture, short of training a vision model on one person. Language is the human-native interface that already scales. Where a picture is needed, a chart, a diagram or a model in three dimensions can be rendered alongside the text.

Strip the how out and what remains is the what, made available: data, and the capabilities to act on it, exposed through an API, an MCP server, an agent-to-agent protocol or direct access to the data layer. In effect, a store and a pipeline over it, with the rendering done at the other end, in whichever harness and model the user happens to be working through. The screen moves to the far end of the wire.

From there it is tempting to take one more step, and I took it myself when I first wrote this argument down: users will stop caring about the vendor's interface and its brand, and will care only whether the store holds the data they want, in the structure they want, reachable the way they want. That is half right. The half that is wrong is where the money is.

Defaults hold. The best-known demonstration of why concerns organ donation, which I offer as an illustration, not as evidence about software. Johnson and Goldstein found effective consent to donation of 4.25% to 27.5% in European countries where citizens had to opt in, against 85.9% to 99.98% where they had to opt out (Johnson and Goldstein, 2003, p.1338). In their online experiment, where respondents "could at a mouse click change their choice", the "Revealed donation rates were about twice as high when opting-out as when opting-in": 82% consented when donation was the default, 42% when it was not, and 79% when there was no default and they simply had to choose (p.1338). They give three reasons a default sticks. People may believe "that defaults are suggestions by the policy-maker, which imply a recommended action". Deciding takes effort, "whereas accepting the default is effortless". And the default is the status quo, the reference point against which any change is judged, and since "losses loom larger than the equivalent gains", leaving it feels like a loss (p.1338). None of the three is about organs. Each applies to any configuration that somebody else has chosen for you.

In software the effect is no illustration, and it has been priced at the scale harness vendors work at. In the United States' search case against Google, the District Court for the District of Columbia accepted the plaintiffs' expert's evidence that "defaults have a powerful impact on consumer decisions" (*United States v Google LLC*, 2024, p.30). It found that "In 2021, Google paid out a total of $26.3 billion in revenue share under these contracts", the contracts that bought those placements (p.105), and concluded that "All of this makes the defaults extremely valuable" (p.232). Nobody pays $26.3 billion in a single year for something its users are about to stop caring about. And at the remedies stage the same court declined to bar Google "from making payments or offering other consideration to distribution partners for preloading or placement" of its products (*United States v Google LLC*, 2025, p.8), so paying for the default is still allowed, which every user and operator should be keenly aware of.

Defaults hold often, though, not always, and both sources say where the hold gives out. The court found that "defaults are less effective when the alternative has strong brand recognition and product quality" (*United States v Google LLC*, 2024, p.212). Johnson and Goldstein's own data show the same limit from another side. The default all but decided who registered: across their countries "nearly 60 percentage points separate the two groups" (Johnson and Goldstein, 2003, p.1339). What finally happened moved far less. Where donation was the default they estimated "a 16.3% (P < 0.02) increase in donation", because families object, doctors hesitate and donors fail to match recipients, whatever the register says (p.1339). A default governs what people sign up to much more than what they end up with. That is a real limit. It is also no comfort to anyone who does not own the default, because often is enough to be worth a great deal to whoever sets it.

In the arrangement I described above, the default is set by the harness: the client in which a person meets the model, and which decides, before they have decided anything, which model answers, which tools are to hand and how an answer is laid out. ChatGPT and Claude Desktop are harnesses in this sense, and both are highly opinionated ones. Each is a coherent product with its own view of how a conversation with a model should go, and a meaningful number of the people who use them will take that view as it comes. So the user interface has not left software. It has moved from the vendor of the data to the vendor of the harness, where it now frames every service the user reaches, and that is a material advantage to the harness vendor.

Users do stop caring about the data vendor's UX, then. They stop because the harness's UX has replaced it. The lowest-common-denominator opinion that every vendor used to ship in its own screens is still being shipped; far fewer people are shipping it.

What the harness vendor usually does not own matters just as much. The data, for the most part, belongs to someone else. So do the intermediary capabilities that expose it: the MCP and API servers in front of a store, and the agents a harness reaches over agent-to-agent protocols. The hardware, usually, is someone else's again. The harness owns very little of the what. What it owns is the conversation.

That is an intermediary's position, and we have seen one like it before. A search engine stood between people and the sources they were looking for, ranked those sources and sent the user on to them. The default harness stands in the same place and sends nobody anywhere. It takes the question, goes to the services on the user's behalf and answers in its own voice, so the user may never see a source's own interface at all. It owns the interaction from end to end. The best word I have for that role is interlocutor: the party you are actually talking to, whoever it has been talking to on your behalf. A historical materialist will recognise the structure, and "control of the means of interaction" is a fair name for what the default harness holds.

Actor-network theory has a more exact term, and since the essay needs it later, this is the place to introduce it. Michel Callon showed how an actor that defines a problem in its own terms makes itself "an obligatory passage point" for everyone else the problem involves: the point they all have to go through to get what they want (Callon, 1986). The default harness is an obligatory passage point in that plain sense. It sits between the operator or user and the whole range of services behind it, the data and capabilities reached through MCP and API servers and the agents reached over agent-to-agent protocols, and every one of those services is met through it. I will come back to obligatory passage points later, where they bear on how context itself is formed. For now it is enough to see who is standing in one.

So the value the screen used to hold does not vanish. It concentrates in a few harnesses, while the data and the capabilities, which is to say most of what used to be the product, stay with whoever owns them. For anyone who does not own a harness, software devolves into backend, middleware and integration: the store, the pipeline over it, and the work of making both reachable through somebody else's passage point. The user still cares whether the store holds the data they want, in the structure they want, reachable the way they want. They express that care through an interface the data vendor no longer controls.

That is my working answer to the opening question, and it is not the received view. So I owe the promise I made at the start: who holds the received view, and in what words. The field already has a crowded vocabulary for this shift, and it helps to sort the terms by what each one names: a process, an artefact or a practice.

The process names treat the change as a new lifecycle. AWS calls its version the AI-Driven Development Life Cycle, or AI-DLC (AWS, 2025). "Agentic SDLC" circulates widely with no single author. ADLC is used for two different things, a lifecycle for building agents and a lifecycle in which agents do the building, which is reason enough not to lean on it.

The artefact names say that the thing being made has changed. Karpathy's Software 2.0 and Software 3.0, which I quoted at the start, belong here. So does the academic version, SE 3.0, which proposed "intent-first, conversation-oriented development" with "AI teammates" in October 2024, ahead of the vendor wave of 2025 (Hassan et al., 2024). So does GitHub's Spec Kit, whose launch post describes a shift from "code is the source of truth" to "intent is the source of truth" (Delimarsky, 2025).

The practice names describe what a person now does. Karpathy named "vibe coding" in February 2025, a way of working in which "you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists" (Karpathy, 2025b), and a year later gave his "current favorite" name for what came after it: "agentic engineering" (Karpathy, 2026). Between the two came "context engineering". Tobi Lütke preferred it to prompt engineering because "It describes the core skill better: the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM" (Lütke, 2025), and Karpathy, within the week, called it "the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step" (Karpathy, 2025c).

Names are half of it. The other half is how much each camp thinks has changed, and the opinions run along a spectrum.

At one end is augmentation: the SDLC as it was, with assistive tools bolted on. Its canonical evidence is the controlled experiment on GitHub Copilot in which developers "completed the task significantly faster–55% faster" (Peng et al., 2023). It was one task, writing an HTTP server in JavaScript, done by 95 developers. The result is real, and it is a measurement of one phase of a process that nobody proposed to change.

Next comes compression and reordering: the same phases, run faster, merged or re-sequenced. AWS rejects augmentation in so many words, warning that "Simply retrofitting AI as an assistant not only constrains its capabilities but also reinforces outdated inefficiencies", and announces that "Traditional 'sprints' are replaced by 'bolts' – shorter, more intense work cycles" (AWS, 2025). Yet the AI-DLC keeps its phases, Inception, Construction and Operations, and its AI "implements solutions only after receiving human validation". It calls itself transformative and is, structurally, a compression. Boris Tane sits at the far edge of this position, because for him collapse is not speed-up: "The stages collapsed. They didn't get faster. They merged." What is left is a loop, "Intent. Build. Observe. Repeat." (Tane, 2026). AWS's Kiro reorders rather than removes, pairing "the flow of vibe coding" with "the clarity of specs" and running requirements, then design, then tasks (Swaminathan and Singh, 2025). The 2025 DORA report gives the position its empirical form: "AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there" (Harvey and DeBellis, 2025).

Then transformation: a new paradigm, in which the artefact, the unit of work or the roles change. Software 3.0 is the clearest statement of it, SE 3.0 the academic one and Spec Kit's "intent is the source of truth" the vendor one. Patrick Debois puts it as plainly as anyone: "The object moving through the pipeline isn't code. It's knowledge and intent." (Debois, 2026). Anthropic's own data points the same way, with "79% of conversations on Claude Code were identified as 'automation'", against 49% on Claude.ai, and its authors speculate that developers "will shift to mostly managing and guiding these systems, rather than writing code themselves" (Anthropic, 2025). That last is speculation, and the source says so.

Against all three stand the sceptics, and theirs is the most uncomfortable data in the field. METR's 2025 study found experienced open-source developers 19% slower with AI tools (METR, 2025). In February 2026 METR labelled that result "out of date", reported a new estimate of an 18% speed-up (CI −38% to +9%), and called the new figure "an unreliable signal" (METR, 2026). The 2024 DORA report found that "As AI adoption increased, it was accompanied by an estimated decrease in delivery throughput by 1.5%, and an estimated reduction in delivery stability by 7.2%" (DORA, 2024): correlational survey estimates, per 25% increase in adoption. By 2025 the throughput relationship had turned positive, but "AI adoption does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability", and with 90% of respondents using AI, "30% report little or no trust in the code generated by AI" (Harvey and DeBellis, 2025). GitClear, a vendor, so read it with that in mind, reports from 211 million changed lines a "4x Growth in Code Clones", with cloned lines rising "from 8.3% to 12.3%" between 2021 and 2024 and moved lines, the trace of refactoring, falling "from 25% of changed lines in 2021, to less than 10% in 2024" (GitClear, 2025).

A fifth reading treats all of this as one more abstraction layer, the next step after assembler, high-level languages and frameworks. Martin Fowler accepts the scale of the change, putting it at "a similar degree as the change from assembler" to high-level languages, and then refuses the comfort of the analogy by quoting Birgitta Böckeler: "we're moving *sideways* into non-determinism at the same time" (Böckeler, quoted in Fowler, 2025). Fortran kept in version control compiles to the same behaviour every time. A prompt kept in version control does not reliably reproduce it.

A sixth reading is about labour. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab finds that "employment of young workers (ages 22–25) in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with that of their less-exposed peers", working "primarily through reduced hiring", and calls its own results "early, descriptive indicators… rather than causal estimates" (Brynjolfsson et al., 2026a). Anthropic's trial of 52 developers, "mostly junior", found a "statistically significant decrease in mastery" among those using AI assistance, with quiz scores of 50% against 67%, no significant gain in speed and the largest gap on debugging (Shen and Tamkin, 2026). The worry here is what happens to people, and to skill, when the work that trained them is done by something else. I will return to it.

The seventh reading is less a position than a pattern in the evidence: the work is moving into review and verification. DORA warns that "acceleration can expose weaknesses downstream" (Harvey and DeBellis, 2025). The AI-DLC makes the human a validator, with the AI implementing "only after receiving human validation" (AWS, 2025). GitClear's clones are, among other things, more code for somebody to read. Taken together, they say the human effort is shifting from doing the work towards checking it.

The received view I began with lives at the first two points on that spectrum: the same lifecycle, with assistance added or its slow parts compressed. I have said I think it is wrong. But every position on the spectrum is held by serious people with dated evidence, and any claim to succeed the SDLC has to say where it stands among them.

So here is where the CELC stands. The Context Engineering Lifecycle, the CELC, sits at the transformation end of the spectrum, and it makes two claims there that, as far as I can find (I may be wrong, or have overlooked a neighbour), none of its neighbours makes together.

The first is a claim of succession. Software itself is being redefined, from a what bundled with a how into data and capabilities rendered in somebody else's harness, and a lifecycle built to produce the old thing cannot simply be run faster to produce the new one. It has to be succeeded. The second is a claim of conservation. Fred Brooks distinguished the essence of software, the difficulties inherent in its nature, from its accidents, and held that essential complexity grows non-linearly with size (Brooks, 1987). Generating code does not make that essential complexity disappear. It moves it, into context. That is the thesis the rest of this essay argues, and it has to be earned further on, along with the questions it leaves open: whether some complexity does in fact vanish on the way, and what stops context, once it carries the complexity, from fragmenting the way code did.

By context I mean something with two aspects, and the CELC needs both. Context is the medium through which the builder, the engineer or the operator now directs computation. That is not a break with programming. Directing computation is what every programming language has ever done, and Karpathy's "your prompts are now programs that program the LLM" (Karpathy, 2025a) puts the new medium in the same line as the old ones. Context is simply the newest of them. Context is also the thing that has to be managed: it is produced, kept, corrected and superseded, and it goes wrong when nobody does those things. A lifecycle through context and a lifecycle of context are the same lifecycle, seen once from the computation it directs and once from the material it is made of.

That second aspect is why I keep the term "context engineering", with one correction. Context and the context window are different things, and they should not be conflated. Context is the unit of input being managed, and it comes in many forms. The window is the gateway through which context reaches a model for inference, and "window" is the better word for that. Tobi Lütke's definition already keeps the two apart, since it never mentions the window: "the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM" (Lütke, 2025).

The window is also, invariably, an obligatory passage point. Whatever form context takes, it reaches the model only through the window. So there is a second passage point behind the one I described earlier: the operator or user goes through the harness, context goes through the window, and the harness decides what reaches the window. It is a chain, and the harness stands at the front of it.

Karpathy's definition names the window, because it describes the moment of filling it: context engineering is "the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step" (Karpathy, 2025c). In the same post he lists what an LLM application has to do "On top of context engineering itself": "break up problems just right into control flows", "pack the context windows just right", "dispatch calls to LLMs of the right kind and capability", "handle generation-verification UIUX flows", and "a lot more - guardrails, security, evals, parallelism, prefetching", which is why, for him, "context engineering is just one small piece of an emerging thick layer of non-trivial software that coordinates individual LLM calls (and a lot more) into full LLM apps". I do not read that against him. Once context and the window are kept apart, his thick layer describes the same thing I do, and anyone who has built a data platform will recognise its shape. The control flows are ETL pipelines, with context extracted, transformed and loaded. Those pipelines have parameters that need adjusting, which is to say configuration, and that is where the rest of his list lives. Packing the windows is indexing. Dispatching calls to the right model is data routing. The generation-verification flows are the display layer an analyst works from, the dashboard. Guardrails and evals are the data rule engines. The analogy fits.

The CELC is a label for the lifecycle of that work, and only a label: not a new coinage, and nothing about it is proprietary. The phrase is already in use. The earliest use I have found is a post on the OneReach.ai blog, published on 8 September 2025, which captions a figure "The Context Engineering Lifecycle" and says that "The context engineering lifecycle consists of seven critical stages": "Ingest", "Normalize", "Index", "Retrieve", "Compose", "Evaluate" and "Persist" (Slesarenko, 2025). It is not an authoritative source, and I do not cite it as one. The term is nascent, and nothing written about it is authoritative yet. Read in order, though, those seven stages describe a pipeline for context, which sits comfortably with the ETL analogy above.

The nearest neighbour is Patrick Debois's Context Development Lifecycle, the CDLC, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the CELC is the CDLC under another name. Debois starts where I do: "if context is the new bottleneck, then we need a development lifecycle built around it". He treats context "as an engineering artifact (something we generate, evaluate, distribute, and observe)", from which "a lifecycle emerges" (Debois, 2026). Nor is his ambition small. He expects the CDLC to "reshape how we think about software development as profoundly as DevOps reshaped how we think about delivery and operations" (Debois, 2026). His account of context that goes stale and contradicts itself is close to the one I give later.

The line between us runs through both aspects of context, and through the claim. Take the object first. The CDLC's context is an artefact developed alongside code and in its service. It "needs the same engineering rigour we eventually gave to code: version control, testing, security, distribution, monitoring, and continuous improvement", and the post closes on a question: "You've invested in getting agents to write code. What have you invested in giving them the context to write it well?" (Debois, 2026). That is context as something with a lifecycle, and it is half of what I mean. The other half is context as the medium through which computation is now directed, and what it directs is more than agents writing code. It directs the software itself: the data and capabilities left over, as I argued at the start, once the how is stripped out. Then the claim. DevOps, the analogy Debois chooses, reshaped how we think about delivery and operations by joining two halves of one lifecycle, and the lifecycle it joined carried on. A lifecycle that reshapes software development as DevOps did sits alongside the lifecycle it reshapes. The CELC claims more, and so has more to prove: the SDLC was the lifecycle of a particular thing, that thing has changed, and so the lifecycle is succeeded, not supplemented. This is my answer to the first objection, that the CELC is only the CDLC renamed: it would land if the CELC were only a lifecycle of context.

The second objection is that all of this is one more abstraction layer, and in one respect I have just conceded it. If context is the newest medium for directing computation, it stands in a line with assembler, high-level languages and frameworks. What the objection misses is the direction of the step. Böckeler's point, as Fowler quotes it, is that "we're moving *sideways* into non-determinism at the same time" (Böckeler, quoted in Fowler, 2025). The abstraction layers the objection has in mind form a ladder, each rung reducing reliably to the one below, so the lifecycle that managed the program could carry on as it was, one rung up. A sideways step into non-determinism gives no such guarantee, and it is taken while the thing being directed is itself changing, from a bundled what and how into data and capabilities. A new rung would leave the SDLC standing. A step off the ladder, under a product that is changing shape, is why the lifecycle has to change.

The third objection is that the productivity is illusory, and the sceptics' data I set out above are its case. I read the same data as complexity left unmanaged in its new location. GitClear's rising clones and falling moved lines (GitClear, 2025) are what generated code looks like when nobody refactors it: the structure that used to be maintained in code is now maintained nowhere. DORA's finding that AI adoption "does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability" (Harvey and DeBellis, 2025) is the same cost arriving downstream. And METR's 2025 study, whose 19% slowdown METR has since labelled "out of date" in favour of an estimate it calls "an unreliable signal" (METR, 2026), reported something more telling than its headline figure: "developers expected AI to speed them up by 24%, and even after experiencing the slowdown, they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%" (METR, 2025). A gap that wide between how the work felt and how it measured is what you would expect if part of the work had moved somewhere the developers were not looking. None of this proves the shift. It is consistent with it, and it says where the managing has to be done.

The fourth objection is the most serious. It comes from Rich Sutton's bitter lesson: that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin", and that "We have to learn the bitter lesson that building in how we think we think does not work in the long run" (Sutton, 2019). Rachel Laycock and her colleagues on Thoughtworks's Technology Advisory Board turned it on spec-driven development in their *Technology Radar* of November 2025: "We may be relearning a bitter lesson — that handcrafting detailed rules for AI ultimately doesn't scale" (Laycock et al., 2025, p.19). If the CELC asked organisations to write their context by hand, scale would make it redundant.

On the empirical point, Sutton and the Radar's authors are right. Handcrafted context does not scale. I have watched it fail on projects I have worked on, and the hands-on trials I know of point the same way. Birgitta Böckeler, who is also one of those authors, tried Kiro and spec-kit herself and came away "very skeptical that lots of up-front spec design is a good idea"; faced with the output for a small bug, she would "rather review code than all these markdown files" (Böckeler, 2025). Colin Eberhardt, after "2,577 lines of markdown" and "3.5 hrs review time", was "a lot more productive without SDD, around ten times faster" (Eberhardt, 2025). That is two practitioners and a handful of tools, and they agree with the Radar. A year on, the Radar has moved a different kind of tool into Trial. OpenSpec is "a lightweight spec-driven development framework that works with any coding agent" that "centers on a spec-anchored workflow", and "Teams report more predictable agent output and fewer unintended changes compared to bare prompting" (Laycock et al., 2026, p.43). Where the spec persists alongside the code, it seems to fare better than a spec written once, up front. Context, though, does not have to be handcrafted. It behaves like data, and it can be handled like data: extracted, transformed and loaded. Sutton leaves room for exactly this. He writes that "we should build in only the meta-methods that can find and capture this arbitrary complexity" (Sutton, 2019), and the ETL of context is such a meta-method.

Most of the context a business needs, it already produces. Its day-to-day operations generate records that would exist anyway: tickets, documents, conversations, the trail its decisions leave. Until now, making those records usable by anyone else took an expert to interpret them, a person who stood as an obligatory passage point between the records and everyone who needed what was in them, and whose time limited how much got through. A model can now stand where that expert stood. It does not tire, does not take time off, and works at speed. That is a substitution, plainly.

What a business keeps its own hand on is narrower: the portion of context that carries its business rules, its compliance obligations, its brand and its taste, where it wants control over exactly what the model is given and where judgement stays with people. This portion can be curated by hand because it is small. It lives alongside the far larger body of context fed programmatically from the records of daily operations, and the two do not exclude each other. The bitter lesson holds for the handcrafted part. It is the smaller part.

So the CELC stands among its neighbours at the transformation end of the spectrum, claiming succession and conservation, for a context that is at once the medium of computation and the thing to be managed. The claim of succession rests on more than I have shown so far. To see why the SDLC cannot simply be run faster, it helps to see what the SDLC and its variants were for, and that is where I turn next.

## Every method is an opinion about complexity

The SDLC has never been one thing. It has been a succession of methods, each arriving with its own vocabulary and its own advocates: waterfall, which in hindsight was an early form of spec-driven development; the V-model, which paired each stage of specification with a stage of testing; Scrum, and the wider Agile family it belongs to; Extreme Programming, with test-driven development at its core; the twelve-factor app; DevSecOps. They are usually presented as process, as ways of organising work. I read each of them as an opinion: a view about how to stop the complexity of functional code from cascading past what the people responsible for it can manage.

The opinions differ on where the cascade can be stopped. Waterfall and the V-model stop it before the code exists, by deciding the system's parts and their connections up front and checking the result against that decision. XP stops it at the smallest unit, pinning each piece of behaviour with a test written before the code, so that a change which ripples further than intended shows up as a failure. Scrum and Agile stop it in time, by keeping each increment small enough to understand and cheap enough to throw away. The twelve-factor app stops it at the boundary between an application and the environment it runs in, and DevSecOps at the boundary between building software and securing and running it. Their advocates would describe them in other terms, as ways of serving customers, coping with requirements that change or shipping safely, and they would not be wrong. But a changed requirement ends as a change to code, and what it costs is set by how much of the code each change disturbs. Every method on the list is a bet about where that cost can be contained.

Each method also works best at a certain size, and grows heavier past it. A specification one architect could hold becomes a library nobody reads end to end. A test suite that ran while the kettle boiled runs overnight. A stand-up for six becomes a ceremony for sixty. The method does not fail all at once. It becomes overweight, as more of a team's effort goes into feeding it and less into what it was meant to protect. The usual response has been to cut the codebase down to a size the method can still serve, and I will come to where that response led. First, though, it is worth being exact about why the complexity grows faster than the code.

Start with the functional layer, code to code. Complexity lives in the edges between units, not in the lines of code. A thousand lines that call nothing else are easy to reason about. N functions, each of which could call, share state with or modify any other, give N(N−1)/2 potential pairwise interactions: 45 for 10 functions, 499,500 for 1,000. That growth is polynomial, quadratic in N, and it is the unconstrained worst case. Real dependency graphs are much sparser than that, and making them sparse is what architecture is for. In 1972 David Parnas argued that the criteria by which a system is decomposed into modules decide how modular it turns out to be, and proposed information hiding as the criterion (Parnas, 1972). The discipline that grew from it bounds each unit's connections, so that the number of edges grows close to linearly with the number of units. Complexity grows quadratically when nothing constrains coupling. Architecture is the discipline of constraining it.

Shared mutable state is worse. k flags or variables that interact give 2^k reachable states: 1,024 for ten flags, more than a million for twenty. This is where "exponential" is the right word, and it is the word I keep for it. Each new unit adds edges in proportion to the units already there. Each new interacting flag doubles the states.

None of that arithmetic is a measurement. It describes the shape of the risk, not its size, and the folk versions of it, which put a precise multiple on how much harder each new feature makes a system, claim more than anyone has measured. The defensible empirical frame is Meir Lehman's laws of software evolution, and his second law is the one that matters here: "As an evolving program is continually changed, its complexity, reflecting deteriorating structure, increases unless work is done to maintain or reduce it." (Lehman, 1980, p.1068). The weight of the sentence is on its last clause. Lehman does not say how fast complexity grows. He says it does not stop growing by itself, and that holding it back takes work. Organising that work is what the methods were for.

That is half the picture. Code is written and changed by people, and the people form a network of their own, with edges of their own. Software is, among other things, a distillation of what those people understand of the problem it solves, and the most radical account of that understanding is Peter Naur's.

In "Programming as Theory Building" Naur argued that "the proper, primary aim of programming is, not to produce programs, but to have the programmers build theories", with documentation "an auxiliary, secondary product" (Naur, 1985, p.253). He then went as far as the argument can go. The theory, he wrote, "is something that could not conceivably be expressed, but is inextricably bound to human beings". He held that "program revival, that is reestablishing the theory of a program merely from the documentation, is strictly impossible", and that the death of a program "happens when the programmer team possessing its theory is dissolved" (p.258).

Anyone proposing a lifecycle built on context has to face that page. Agents act on what can be given to them, and Naur says that what matters most about a program cannot be given to anyone in writing. If he is right in the way he meant it, the CELC is an attempt at the strictly impossible.

I think Naur was right about how programs die and wrong about where their theory lives, and the way to see both is through a movement that three traditions have named, each from its own side. The network that made something drops out of sight, and what is left is a thing that simply works. Historical materialism calls this "reification", after Georg Lukács, for whom "a relation between people takes on the character of a thing and thus acquires a 'phantom objectivity'", an autonomy that conceals "every trace of its fundamental nature: the relation between people" (Lukács, 1971). The stress there falls on the labour and the relations that are forgotten. Actor-network theory has two names for it. Bruno Latour's "black-boxing" stresses that the network becomes invisible because it succeeds: once a thing runs reliably, attention goes to its inputs and outputs and not to its insides (Latour, 1999). Michel Callon's and John Law's "punctualisation" stresses that a whole network comes to act as a single point (Callon, 1991; Law, 1992). My own thesis runs the first and the last together, calling a simplification "a reified understanding or in the context of this work, punctualized" (Li, 2020, p.68), and glosses the middle one as "intentionally making opaque the inner workings of technologies so as to reduce the complexity of comprehending them to inputs and resultant outputs" (p.87). They are not quite synonyms. They are the same movement, seen from different sides. Of the three, black-boxing is the one most readers already know, from engineering as much as from sociology, and it is the one I will use from here on.

Read that way, a program, its documentation included, is black-boxed theory. It works, and it can no longer be traced back to the thinking that produced it. The documentation does carry some of the context that passed between the members of the team, but it is part of the box, not a key to it. Naur is right that the box cannot be reopened from its contents, or not at any cost worth paying, a point I return to later. Where I part from him is over who he thinks was inside. He places nearly all the weight on the people in the team, and leaves out everything else that took part.

Actor-network theory gives good reason to include the rest. Its practitioners work from a principle that Callon first named, "generalised symmetry", in which, as I put it in my doctoral thesis, "all of the actants and objects that lend effect to the construction, or translation of an assemblage or actor network, are treated as ontologically equal" (Li, 2020, p.152). People are among those things. They are not the only ones. A programmer's theory of a program was never theirs alone. It was built on what they were taught, on the code they inherited, on the documents and tickets they read and the conversations they had, on the sensibilities of the people who worked on the problem before them. Nor was it ever held apart from the things it was built with. What is a programmer without the hardware, the software and the language they work in? Languages, compilers, frameworks, build tools, environments and machines do not carry a team's intentions through unchanged. In Latour's terms they are "mediators", which transform what passes through them, as opposed to intermediaries, which carry it unaltered (Latour, 2005), and an actor, in his phrase, is "what is made to act by a large star-shaped web of mediators flowing in and out of it" (Latour, 2005, p.217, quoted in Li, 2020, p.71). Those mediators hold part of the context too, and when the team dissolves they tend to go with it, as versions move on and environments are retired. That is why the trace cannot be recovered. The theory is a property of the whole heterogeneous network, of the people, the code, the documents, the conversations and the tickets, of the tools they worked through, and now of the harnesses and models that sit among them.

A large language model makes the point almost too neatly. It is built, quite literally, on a network of knowledge external to it, and it comes close to being the quintessential "actor-network", a thing which, as my thesis glosses the term, "whether it is seen as a self-determining actor or a network of a confluence of effects is dependent on perspective, and it is not reducible to either just an actor or just a network" (Li, 2020, p.80). A model answers as a single actor, a black box in the plainest sense. It was made, and it works, as a network.

Read Naur's p.258 again with that in mind and most of it holds. He is right that a program cannot be revived "merely from the documentation", because documentation is one element of the network, and revival from it alone asks one element to do the work of all of them. He is right that programs die when their teams dissolve. What he describes as death is a network coming apart: the human links that held it together are cut, the mediators they worked through are lost with them, and what remains no longer adds up to a theory of anything. It comes apart for a reason Naur did not name. Once a program is black-boxed it is necessarily precarious, because the network inside the box stays aligned only for as long as somebody keeps it aligned. The box looks stable, though, and that stability is an illusion which discourages the very maintenance it depends on. The irony is that the box is not inert in this: simply by working, it argues that nothing needs doing. So when a link inside it does fail, in a gap where any substitute is shallow, there is nothing left to repair it from. The program becomes unrecoverable, which is Naur's death, re-described. The one claim that does not hold is the one that binds theory to human beings alone, and it hides a blind spot, because a program can die from the non-human side as well. Every engineer knows the familiar case: a dependency that stops being maintained, or that updates in a way that breaks the program around it. Models and harnesses are mediators too, and just as perishable, deprecated by their vendors or changed under the people who rely on them. Whether that is the old risk or a new scale of it is a question I come back to when I turn to how context ages. The CELC, on this reading, is not an attempt to write the theory down, and Naur is right that it cannot be written down in the sense he meant. It is the practice of keeping the network that holds the theory assembled, with context and models among its members, and of treating the loss of any member, human or not, as a standing risk to be built for.

That is the theory of it. The practice is the division I drew earlier, in answering the bitter lesson. A model now does what the expert mediator did: it interprets the records a business produces in its daily operations and renders them usable by everyone else. Human judgement stays obligatory in the narrow, governed portion, in the business rules, the compliance obligations, the brand and the taste that a business keeps its own hand on. That is not a concession that theory lives in people after all. It describes where the network routes certain decisions, not where its theory resides. People matter to a program's theory, as Naur said. They are not the only things that hold it.

The team that holds a program's theory is also a network in the plainest arithmetical sense, and Fred Brooks did the sum. Developers who each need to talk to every other have n(n−1)/2 communication paths between them: one for two developers, 45 for ten. That overhead, together with the time newcomers take to learn what the others already know, is what sits behind his law: "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" (Brooks, 1975). It is the same unconstrained worst case as on the functional layer, and it is cut down the same way. Team structures, the interfaces between teams and the documents that pass across them exist so that most people never need to talk to most others. They are information hiding for people.

The two networks do not stay apart. Melvin Conway's observation, which Brooks later named Conway's law, is that "organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" (Conway, 1968). Teams that do not talk produce interfaces that do not fit, and the glue code at the seams is where the gaps in the social network show up in the functional one.

For a long time that was an engineer's proverb. It has since been tested. Alan MacCormack, Carliss Baldwin and John Rusnak compared matched pairs of products from tightly and loosely coupled organisations, the loosely coupled ones open-source, and found the loosely coupled organisations' products significantly more modular, by up to a factor of six in how far a change propagates (MacCormack et al., 2012). Nachiappan Nagappan, Brendan Murphy and Victor Basili found, on Windows Vista, that measures of the organisation predicted which parts of the system would fail better than the code metrics did, churn, complexity, coverage and dependencies among them (Nagappan et al., 2008). The shape of the organisation is in the code, and the failures follow it.

Put the two layers together and the trouble compounds. A dense social network laid over a dense functional network asks more of the people in it than anyone can carry. No developer can hold the system's state in their head, and the effects of a single change spread combinatorially, along functional edges and social ones at once, faster than any one person can follow. As I put it in my thesis, about a different kind of network, "the human mind is often unable to detect network complexity, much less comprehend it on an infinite scale" (Li, 2020, p.86). What follows is familiar to anyone who has looked after a large estate: technical debt, bugs and deployments that come further and further apart. Every method I listed at the start is, in effect, a strategy for keeping one or both of these networks sparse.

When the methods grew overweight, the answer was to cut the system up until each piece was small enough for a method to serve again. That is where microservices came from: a service per team, sized so the team could hold it, behind an interface the other teams could rely on without looking inside. It was black-boxing on purpose. Naur's programs were black-boxed by default, as a side effect of the work; a microservice is black-boxed by design, its insides hidden so that everyone else can treat it as inputs and outputs. For each team, for a while, it worked.

Then the boxes multiplied. Callon's word is the exact one for what each service became: "The process of punctualisation thus converts an entire network into a single point or node in another network" (Callon, 1991). Each service was a network of code, people and decisions folded into a node, and the estate was a network of those nodes. The arithmetic of the functional and social layers did not go away. It moved up a level, from functions to services and from developers to teams, and it moved to where fewer people were looking. In the engineering organisations that went this way, each team could see inside its own box and the interfaces of its neighbours, and nobody could trace the network the boxes made between them. Adding architects to watch over it did not bring the view back, because each of them watched from inside a part. The risks the methods had been meant to remove returned at the seams, where nobody owned them. In actor-network terms the black-boxed state is "highly precarious", carrying "the risk of subversion, and the encapsulated network of associations degenerating into a mess of naked, failing networks" (Li, 2020, p.87). It is a fair description of a large microservice estate on a bad night.

Some organisations have since pulled their services back together, into fewer of them and into a single repository. It is tempting to read that as the pendulum swinging back, monolith against microservice, as though the argument had been about how many boxes to have. I don't think it ever was. What consolidation bought, where it worked, was an architecture people could understand again: fewer boxes, fewer seams, a network somebody could trace from end to end. Consolidation was the means. Understandability was the objective, and it had been the objective of every method on the list. Waterfall's specification, XP's tests, Scrum's small team and the microservice's small service were each a way of keeping a system within what its people could understand.

That is the ground the rest of this essay stands on. If every method was, in the end, a way of keeping software understandable to the people responsible for it, then the question for a lifecycle of generated software is not how quickly it produces code. It is where the complexity goes once people no longer write the code, and what keeps it understandable when it gets there. I will argue that it goes into context. Before that, there is an older story to tell, about what happens to the organisation of work when the thing that powers it changes.

## From the craft shop to the dynamo

The thing came before its name, and for a while it was named for what it was not. "Automobile" reached English from French, built from the Greek *autos*, self, and the Latin *mobilis*, movable. Before that word settled, the thing was a motor carriage, a steam carriage or a horseless carriage: a carriage with the horse taken away. Henry Ford's first, which ran in 1896, was the Quadricycle, named for its four bicycle wheels, and Ford called it his "gasoline buggy" (Ford and Crowther, 1922). By 1922, when he wrote his memoir, the horseless carriage belonged to the years in which it "was considered merely a freak notion" (Ford and Crowther, 1922).

We are in the horseless-carriage years of software. Look again at the vocabulary I sorted at the start: AI-assisted development, the AI-Driven Development Life Cycle, the agentic SDLC. Each names the new thing as the old thing with something added: development, with assistance; the lifecycle, with AI in front of it. I use "AI-assisted development" myself, for the practice as it stands, because that is what it still is. A name like that records what its users still take for granted. The horseless carriage took the carriage for granted.

The name lagged the thing. So did the organisation of the work around it. I will read the history of the motor industry the way a historical materialist reads any history of production. Each of its four stages, craft, Ford, Sloan and Toyota, was a change in the forces of production, the tools, machines and methods with which the work was done, and each eventually reorganised the relations of production: who does the work, who controls the process and what counts as skill. I give each stage its due, because AI-assisted development is a change of the same kind, and the question is which reorganisation it brings.

Before Ford, cars were hand-built in small numbers by skilled craftspeople. Parts made in different shops did not quite fit, so fitters filed them down until they did; in the case James Womack, Daniel Jones and Daniel Roos describe, the body came from a Paris coachbuilder; no two cars were exactly alike; and most of the workers had come up through an apprenticeship (Womack et al., 1990, pp.21–26). The relations of production followed from the forces. The skilled worker held the knowledge of how the thing went together, set the pace at which it did, and was the one who could say whether it was right. Skill meant fitting, the trained judgement that made this part meet that one. The product could be superb. It was also slow and expensive, and it depended on people who had served apprenticeships.

The traditional SDLC is the craft shop. Engineers write code line by line, fitting bespoke logic, architectures and workarounds to each application, and the theory of the thing lives in the network of people and tools I described above. Craft will persist, and it should. For the gnarliest problems, the ones nobody has solved before, the craft shop can still build the best possible vehicle for one particular purpose, and the engineers who can work that way will be well paid for it. It will not be how most software is made. Craft manages its unit, functional code, at a finer grain than the work now needs, once much of that unit can be generated. Choosing not to automate, or to automate less, is now an opinion, not a requirement, which puts craft in the same company as every method on the list above.

Ford changed the forces of production by breaking the fitting into steps. In his own account, the best his plant had done at stationary assembly was "an average of twelve hours and twenty-eight minutes per chassis". Pulling the chassis down a line by rope and windlass cut that to "five hours fifty minutes", and in early 1914 a raised line and "a further subdivision of work so that each man had fewer movements cut down the labour time per chassis to one hour thirty-three minutes" (Ford and Crowther, 1922). Those are hours of labour per chassis, not the time it took to build a car, and the difference matters, because what fell was the human labour in each one. The assembly of the motor, "formerly done by one man, is now divided into eighty-four operations" (Ford and Crowther, 1922). That is how a luxury became a mass product.

Ford was frank about where the skill went. The principles of the line, he wrote, produced "the reduction of the necessity for thought on the part of the worker and the reduction of his movements to a minimum", so that the worker "does as nearly as possible only one thing with only one movement". He denied that he had taken skill out of work: "We have not. We have put in skill. We have put a higher skill into planning, management, and tool building, and the results of that skill are enjoyed by the man who is not skilled" (Ford and Crowther, 1922). That is the reorganisation in a sentence. Skill did not vanish. It moved off the floor, into the offices that planned the line and the tool rooms that built it, and whoever designed the line now set its pace. The best-known line in the memoir belongs here too: "Any customer can have a car painted any colour that he wants so long as it is black." Ford placed the remark in 1909, writing in 1922, and Model Ts had in fact come in other colours until about 1914. It is a founder's retrospective, polished. It is still a fair summary of what the line did to the product: one chassis, one model, one way.

Alfred P. Sloan's General Motors beat Ford by refusing the one model. Sloan's policy, in his own words, was "a line of cars in each price area, from the lowest price up to one for a strictly high grade, quantity production car" (Sloan, quoted in Tedlow, 1988, pp.55–56), and the range "came to be known as 'the car for every purse and purpose'" (Tedlow, 1988, p.56). GM shared parts beneath its brands and changed its models every year, though "The annual model change… was the innovation no one wanted" (Tedlow, 1988, p.56): flexible mass production, in place of one chassis for every customer. Ford paid for waiting. "Ford's market share collapsed to under 10% in 1927" (Tedlow, 1988, p.51). On 26 May that year Henry and Edsel Ford drove the fifteen-millionth Model T out of the Highland Park plant, which ceremonially ended its production (The Henry Ford, n.d.a). The changeover to the Model A meant "the replacement of some 15,000 machine tools, the total rebuilding of another 25,000" (Sward, quoted in Tedlow, 1988, p.51), and the new car was introduced on 2 December 1927 (The Henry Ford, n.d.b). The forces of production had changed again, and the relations changed with them. Variety had to be planned, timed and paid for, and what counted as skill moved again, towards the people who could manage variety at scale: who decided the range, which parts could be shared and which had to differ, and when each model would change.

AI-assisted development went from Ford to Sloan almost at once. The arrangement I described at the start, with the rendering done in the user's harness, is already Sloanist. A foundation model is the shared chassis. On top of it, variations are prompted, configured and skinned for niches, for departments and in the end for individual users, and rendered in whichever harness the user works through. That is mass customisation: the speed of mass production without giving up the tailoring, down to the person at the far end of the wire. Nobody selling software now would say the equivalent of Ford's line about black. The skill that rises in value is Sloan's, the management of variety, and it sits, as it did at GM, with whoever controls the chassis.

Toyota changed the forces of production in another direction. The Toyota Production System, as Taiichi Ohno set it out, stood on two pillars: just-in-time, making only what is needed when it is needed, and *jidoka*, usually rendered in English as autonomation, in which machines and lines are built to stop at the first abnormality so that its cause can be found and fixed (Ohno, 1988). Any worker on the line could stop it on seeing a defect. Quality was built in where the work was done, instead of inspected in at the end.

Read as relations of production, this is the most interesting of the four stages, because it moved some control back onto the floor. A worker who can stop the line holds an authority over its pace that Ford's worker never had. A line that stops at each abnormality also learns from each one, since every defect becomes the occasion to find its cause and change the process: a loop that runs from the floor back into the design of the work. This is the stage I keep. The learning loop and built-in quality are what the CELC takes from the motor industry, and I will come back to both.

Toyota did not travel as Toyota, though. In the West it travelled as lean, and on the way it changed. Mike Parker and Jane Slaughter, writing from the American plants that adopted Toyota's team concept, called what they found there management-by-stress: a system kept under constant pressure, in which any slack is found and removed. In their words, "the operating principle of management-by-stress is to systematically locate and remove such protections", and "The system, including its human elements, operates in a state of permanent stress" (Parker and Slaughter, 1989; see also Parker and Slaughter, 1988). Read that way, the pressure crossed the Pacific more reliably than the authority of the stop cord, or the learning it served. Lean, as the West repositioned it, meant the intensification of work and the cutting of costs, and in that form I refuse it.

Actor-network theory has a word for what happened, and this is where the essay needs it. In my doctoral thesis I put it this way: "How an actor-network expands, influences, consolidates and defends itself is through the process of 'translation'", in which "the associations are negotiated, and always in danger of succumbing to oppositional effects, resulting in 'translation error'; success is finite, always subject to movement" (Li, 2020, p.82). Translation is not transmission. A method that moves from one network into another is reassembled there, out of whatever the new network will hold. Lean is Toyota translated into the networks of Western management, and the authority of the stop cord was the part those networks would not hold. That is a translation error in the exact sense. A historical materialist would put the same point structurally: a technique that moves between two sets of relations of production is adopted on the terms of the relations that receive it. Here the two lenses I named at the start pull against each other, as I said they sometimes would. Actor-network theory refuses to treat a structure as given before the network that performs it; historical materialism begins with the structure. On lean they disagree about why, and agree about what happened.

The same translation is under way again, with AI-assisted development as the technique and the same networks receiving it. Read what the people building and selling the tools say, in their own dated words. AWS warns that "Simply retrofitting AI as an assistant not only constrains its capabilities but also reinforces outdated inefficiencies", and offers in place of sprints "shorter, more intense work cycles" (AWS, 2025). Anthropic's researchers speculate, as they say themselves, that developers "will shift to mostly managing and guiding these systems, rather than writing code themselves" (Anthropic, 2025). Karpathy's stated goal is "to claim the leverage from the use of agents but without any compromise on the quality of the software" (Karpathy, 2026). None of these sentences says cut costs, and the last puts quality first in so many words.

Others are franker. Asked by an analyst about "labor arbitrage" in February 2025, Salesforce's chief executive, Marc Benioff, said: "I would say that we definitely have seen a lot of efficiency with engineering… And we're not going to hire any new engineers this year. We're seeing 30% productivity increase on engineering." (Benioff, 2025). Amazon's chief executive, Andy Jassy, wrote in August 2024 that its coding assistant, put to work on Java upgrades, had "saved us the equivalent of 4,500 developer-years of work (yes, that number is crazy but, real)", with the upgrades improving security and reducing infrastructure costs, "providing an estimated $260M in annualized efficiency gains" (Jassy, 2024). Ten months later, writing to staff about generative AI across the whole company, not only its engineers, he was plainer: "We will need fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today, and more people doing other types of jobs", and "in the next few years, we expect that this will reduce our total corporate workforce" (Jassy, 2025). Every figure there is a company's estimate of itself. Even the frank ones mostly speak of efficiency, productivity and hiring, and leave the arithmetic to the reader. The consultancy does the arithmetic. BCG's April 2025 deck for executives promises "5-10% typical cost savings for CIOs", foresees "Fewer layers, faster releases, leaner headcount", and, among its levers for driving adoption, lists "Reduce engineering budgets or headcounts to incentivize GenAI usage" (BCG, 2025, pp.3, 10, 15). Scarcity, as an incentive. Lean would recognise it. Read the whole set through lean's history and the vocabulary is familiar: inefficiency to be removed, cycles made more intense, leverage to be claimed, engineers not hired, headcount made leaner. That vocabulary lands where lean's did, with the leaders, technical and not, who decide what the time it frees is for.

The word that does the work is "productivity". In my honours thesis I followed Sidanius and Pratto in reading meritocracy as a legitimising myth, and the move that matters here is theirs. Whether a belief "is epistemologically false or inaccurate, and consequently, whether or not it is valid, has nothing to do with the belief's power to legitimize inequality"; what sustains it is "the intensity of the person's belief in the truthfulness of the ideology" (Li, 2011, p.28). Productivity works the same way in our industry. The evidence I began with shows how unsettled its measurement is, and the most telling result in it was a belief: METR's developers "expected AI to speed them up by 24%, and even after experiencing the slowdown, they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%" (METR, 2025), in a study whose headline figure METR has since called "out of date" (METR, 2026). A belief that survives its own measurement does not need to be true to do its work.

Harry Braverman said what that work was, half a century before agents. Writing about Taylorism, he argued that management under monopoly capital proceeded by separating conception from execution: the knowledge of how the work is done was taken from the people doing it and lodged with the people who planned it (Braverman, 1974). Ford had said the same thing more cheerfully, with skill put into "planning, management, and tool building" and taken off the floor. Productivity was the name under which that separation passed for common sense, and its power never rested on the numbers. AI-assisted development poses the question again. Execution passes to agents. What is left to people is conception, judgement and oversight, and the question is whether that is treated as the work itself or as the remainder of a cost not yet cut.

So AI-assisted development intensifies work, as lean did, and it does so differently. Lean intensified by filling the slack with more of the same work. Agents intensify by taking away the easy work: the boilerplate, the reuse of functions already written, the implementation of logic somebody else has already decided. That work took little thought, and it was where people recovered between the parts that took a lot. What remains is decision and oversight, all day. Lisanne Bainbridge saw the shape of this in 1983, writing about the automation of industrial processes: "the designer who tries to eliminate the operator still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate", and the monitoring that remains is "very boring but very responsible". Her verdict was blunt. "The human monitor has been given an impossible task." (Bainbridge, 1983, pp.775–776). Karpathy's working day, in which "you are orchestrating agents who do and acting as oversight" (Karpathy, 2026), is that job, and he calls it "the new default".

The first measurement of what that costs people goes by a name that is not clinical. In March 2026 Bedard and colleagues reported, in the *Harvard Business Review*, a survey of 1,488 full-time workers in the United States, about 14% of whom reported symptoms of what the authors call "brain fry". Among workers whose jobs involved high levels of AI oversight, reported mental effort was 14% higher, mental fatigue 12% higher and information overload 19% higher (Bedard et al., 2026). The caveats matter. It is a self-reported survey, run by BCG, which sells AI consulting; "brain fry" is not a clinical term; and the sample is workers in general, not developers. It is a first sign, not the last word, and it points where Bainbridge pointed: the load concentrates in the oversight.

The analogy I find most exact is the assembly line itself, a generation on. The worker who used to insert parts into a chassis now supervises robots that insert them at superhuman speed and do not tire. That is not lighter work. At line speed a mistake cascades faster, through more units before anyone sees it, and every stoppage, planned or not, has a larger blast radius, because more is in motion when the line stops. The effort and the time pressure do not go away. They concentrate in the oversight and the decisions, which is where the human was left. It also makes Toyota's cord harder to pull, since each pull now stops more.

The same tool, then, widens a range with two ends. At one end the time it frees goes into better work, in the same time or less: more of the output reviewed and understood, quality built in where the work is done. At the other, the freed time is taken as savings: fewer people, the same deadlines, and the oversight piled on whoever is left. The honours thesis has names for the two readings of productivity that go with them. As I glossed Sidanius and Pratto there, the distinction is between "Hierarchy-Enhancing (in other words, legitimizing myths which support inequality) and Hierarchy-Attenuating (those that support equality)" legitimising myths (Li, 2011, p.29). Productivity as cost-cutting is the hierarchy-enhancing myth: it moves conception further from execution, and the gains further from the people doing the work. Productivity as better work in the same time is the attenuating one. Cost-cutting is the reading the pitch most readily invites, and some leaders have already taken it: in February 2026 *Fortune* reported a finding that "37% of organizations plan to replace early career roles with AI" (Fore, 2026). It is still a choice. The tool does not land anywhere by itself. People decide where it lands, and the CELC lands at the quality end, treating the cognitive load of oversight as something to manage, not something to absorb.

One question I will raise here and not argue. If juniors learned their trade on exactly the work agents now do, the boilerplate, the small fixes, the logic someone else had already decided, how does the next generation become mid- and late-career engineers? Is the ladder being pulled up for good? The evidence so far is early and mixed. Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations stands 19% below its counterfactual (Brynjolfsson et al., 2026a), though the same authors stress that "the precipitous decline in employment for young software developers is a consequence of various factors" (Brynjolfsson et al., 2026b). At Big Tech, SignalFire found that new graduates "now account for just 7% of hires, with new hires down 25% from 2023" (SignalFire, 2025). And IBM's chief human resources officer said in February 2026: "We are tripling our entry-level hiring, and yes, that is for software developers and all these jobs we're being told AI can do" (LaMoreaux, quoted in Fore, 2026). I leave the question open.

One more episode in the history of production bears on all of this. In 1987 Robert Solow, reviewing a book for the *New York Times Book Review*, remarked that the computer age could be seen everywhere except in the productivity statistics (Solow, 1987). Three years later the economic historian Paul David answered with a precedent. In 1900, he wrote, observers "well might have remarked that the electric dynamos were to be seen 'everywhere but in the productivity statistics!'" (David, 1990, p.356). Factory electrification did not "have an impact on productivity growth in manufacturing before the early 1920s… This was four decades after the first central power station opened for business" (David, 1990, p.357). Four decades is a long time for a technology to be everywhere and to show up nowhere that anyone was measuring.

David's explanation for his period was material. The delay, he argued, came from "the unprofitability of replacing still serviceable manufacturing plants" (David, 1990, p.357). A factory built around a steam engine was built around its line shaft: one engine turning one long shaft, with every machine in the building belted to it, and, because turning very long shafts wasted power, the building itself raised over several storeys to keep them short (David, 1990, p.358). Nobody tears that down while it still works. So for a generation manufacturers compromised. The "'group drive' system", in which electric motors drove groups of machines through shorter shafts, stayed in vogue from the mid-1890s to the eve of the 1920s (David, 1990, pp.357–358). The gain came only with unit drive, a motor for each machine, which freed the layout. Factories could be single-storey and arranged around the flow of the work, not the reach of a shaft (David, 1990, p.358), and about half of the acceleration in total factor productivity in manufacturing between 1919 and 1929 is statistically accounted for by the growth of that secondary motor capacity (David, 1990, p.359). The old plant had to wear out before it was worth replacing. For 1900, that is the right account, and a historical materialist should be glad of it.

The material conditions of this transition are different, and so, I think, is the cause of its lag. Capital is not what is short. In June 2026 Morgan Stanley forecast that AI-related global debt issuance would "more than double to nearly $570 billion in 2026", with four hyperscalers "expected to spend $700 billion in outlays this year" and hyperscaler capital expenditure passing "$1 trillion in 2027" (Reuters, 2026). It could be argued that capital is being directed at AI faster than at any technology before it, enough to draw on money that would otherwise sit in the safe havens; I would put it no more strongly than that. The nearer precedent is not the dynamo but the internet, and the capital sunk into the infrastructure that carried it: dramatic overcapacity, and bets on businesses that were flimsy at best. At the time nobody knew how much capacity would be needed, and the only position anyone took was not to have too little and be left behind. Fear of missing out is a strong motive for companies and their investors. It is only sometimes an existential one.

Capital and material conditions do return, but as a risk on the horizon, not as the cause of the lag today. The production of compute is concentrated in a handful of places. In 2021 all of the world's most advanced chipmaking capacity, "in nodes below 10 nanometers", was in "South Korea (8%) and Taiwan (92%)", which the report's authors called "single points of failure that could be disrupted by natural disasters, infrastructure shutdowns, or international conflicts" (Varas et al., 2021, p.5). Japan and the United States are central further up the chain: three US-based firms then held a combined 85% share in the software tools used to design chips, and Japan over 90% of the market for photoresist processing equipment (Varas et al., 2021, p.41). A conflict in East Asia that drew those countries in could disrupt supply badly enough to create a material crunch. Or this generation of hardware may reach a scaling limit, and the next become prohibitively expensive in both its scale and its sophistication. There are countervailing forces: a crop of startups trying to build competitive alternatives to Nvidia's chips and the moat around CUDA, and models that are increasingly able to optimise the kernels they run on. That last is early. KernelBench found frontier reasoning models "matching the PyTorch baseline in less than 20% of the cases" (Ouyang et al., 2025), and a few months later CUDA-L1, trained by reinforcement learning, reported "an average speedup of x3.12 with a median speedup of x1.42" across the same benchmark's 250 kernels (Li et al., 2025). These are medium- to long-term risks, and they cut both ways.

The resistance is real as well, and it is already showing up in public. In the United States midterm elections of November 2026, local communities "opposed data center-related ballot measures in 40 out of 41 cases" (Capoot, 2026). Add slow training and slow adoption inside organisations, and it is tempting to call all of this the material brake. In my view, unqualified as it may be, it is less an impediment of that kind than a symptom of the same thing as the rest: a lack of imagination about what the technology is for, beyond the data centre that is to be built down the road.

That is where I place the lag. Its cause is mainly a failure of imagination, and the reorganisation it holds up is wider than ways of working. It runs through the infrastructure of society, business, government, media and technology, all of which were built around the old forces of production and all of which have to be rebuilt around the new. That will take longer than the accelerationists expect, and it will probably be faster than the doomers would have hoped. The historical evidence is on the side of speed. Diego Comin and Bart Hobijn, tracing fifteen technologies across 166 countries over two centuries, found that "on average, countries have adopted technologies 45 years after their invention", and that "Newer technologies have been adopted faster than old ones" (Comin and Hobijn, 2010). Generative AI fits the trend. By late 2024 "nearly 40 percent of the U.S. population age 18-64" was using it, and "overall adoption has been faster than either PCs or the internet", though at work it has been only "as fast as the personal computer" (Bick et al., 2024). By the measures we have, this is on track to be the fastest technology transition yet. It certainly feels that way.

A historical materialist should be uneasy calling the cause of a lag imagination, and I am, a little. But the imagination I mean is not a free-floating idea. It is the capacity of people and organisations formed under one set of relations of production to picture another, and it is limited by the relations it has to see past. In David's factories the forces of production outran the relations because the old plant was too valuable to scrap. In ours they outrun them because the old organisation is too familiar to see around. The frame is the same. The material conditions differ, and so does what holds the relations back.

The misstep, while that lasts, is to treat AI as a better steam engine inside the old line shaft. In software the line shaft is the sprint: one rhythm to which every piece of work is belted, with its planning, its review and its release turned at the same speed. Using agents to write code faster inside a standard two-week sprint is group drive, a new motor on the old shaft. AWS's "shorter, more intense work cycles" (AWS, 2025) spin the shaft faster. What happens next is what the evidence I began with predicts: the code piles up faster than review and deployment can absorb it. DORA warns that "acceleration can expose weaknesses downstream" (Harvey and DeBellis, 2025), and Faros, a vendor whose telemetry is correlational and should be read that way, reports that "Median time in review is up 441.5%" (Faros, 2026). The motor got faster. The shaft did not change.

The reorganisation is to take the shaft down: to give each unit of work its own motor, and to arrange the work around its flow instead of around one shared rhythm. In its most ambitious form, that means an engineer directing an army of micro-agents, each with its own feature, its own tests and its own deployment loop. We already have the proliferation, and proliferation is not the problem in itself. The problem is oversight and coordination: of the humans, of the agents, and, it could be argued, of the platforms, networks and data underneath them, which have never been well coordinated or well monitored in general. The microservice estate described above shows the risk. When services multiplied, "The risks the methods had been meant to remove returned at the seams, where nobody owned them." A fleet of agents has seams too, and more of them.

There is no consensus yet on how to coordinate it, only a spectrum of approaches. At one end is complete control: a central orchestrator that delegates every task and checks every result, which is hard to achieve over a system that is probabilistic all the way down. At the other is laissez-faire, teams that let 'er rip, and report figures so outlandish that they read as unbelievable. At AI Engineer Europe in April 2026 Vincent Koc, one of OpenClaw's core maintainers, described a project where "at the peak, we were doing 800 commits a day… there's about 10 to 15 core maintainers all with day jobs", a personal day "Where I hit close to 3,000 commits per day", and a method: "So, we did commit maxing… Smash as many commits as we can." (Koc, 2026). Writing for OpenAI in February 2026, Ryan Lopopolo described an internal beta built "with 0 lines of manually-written code", in which "roughly 1,500 pull requests have been opened and merged with a small team of just three engineers", and where "Humans may review pull requests, but aren't required to." (Lopopolo, 2026). For scale, Nick Arcolano of Jellyfish, looking across some 20 million pull requests, found that going from no use of AI to full adoption roughly doubled the pull requests per engineer (Arcolano, 2025). Doubling is a large effect. It is not three thousand commits in a day. Yubin Kim and Xin Liu's experiments show what is at stake at each end, without settling which is right for any given organisation: agents working in parallel without talking to each other amplified errors "by up to 17.2x", a central orchestrator "contained this amplification to just 4.4x", and on tasks that had to be done in order "every multi-agent variant we tested degraded performance by 39-70%" (Kim and Liu, 2026). Where most organisations land will probably be somewhere in between. Where any one of them should land is a question of method, with benefits, trade-offs and risks to weigh, and this essay leaves it open. It only sets the frame.

David himself warned against pushing his analogy too far. "Computers are not dynamos" (David, 1990, p.360), and agents are not electric motors. The dynamo supplied one undifferentiated thing, power, and the factory could be rebuilt around wherever the power now was. What agents supply is less uniform, and what the reorganisation has to be built around is less obvious. The analogy gives the shape of the change, a technology already everywhere, waiting on a reorganisation of the work around it. It does not give the timing, and it does not say what to build around.

That last question is the one that matters. The factory was rebuilt around where the power now was. A lifecycle for generated software has to be rebuilt around where the complexity now is, because, as I argued above, keeping it understandable is what every method was for. What follows argues that it has moved into context, and asks whether all of it did.

## Where the complexity went

The history I have just told is a historical materialist's, and it explains a good deal: why a change in the forces of production ends in a reorganisation of the relations of production, why the reorganisation lags, and which way the gains are likely to run while it does. What it explains less well is how the reorganisation happens inside any one organisation: which people, documents, tools and now agents are drawn into the new arrangement, in what order, what holds them there and where it comes apart. A history at that altitude can say that factories would be rebuilt around unit drive. It cannot say why one plant's rebuild worked and another's stalled, and it is not meant to. Actor-network theory is meant for little else. From here the essay leans on it, with the material history underneath as the frame.

At lean I said that the two lenses disagree about why and agree about what happened. Since what follows leans on one of them, the disagreement should be stated in full. Actor-network theory is flat. It grants no structure a reality prior to the network that performs it: no class, no market and no organisation chart standing behind the associations and explaining them. The flatness is deliberate, and it is what lets the theory follow a compiler or a model as seriously as a person; as I wrote in my doctoral thesis, "its ontological flatness allows the incorporation of methods that cross intellectual boundaries" (Li, 2020, p.88). It also has a consequence, which the same thesis put plainly: "Power, therefore, is inter-connectivity. One actor-network cannot be said to be 'more powerful' than another, rather, it is simply bound to more associations" (Li, 2020, p.82). A historical materialist will answer that this is precisely what a flat theory cannot see: why some networks come to be bound to more associations than others. The $26.3 billion I cited at the start was not spent on associations at random. Historical materialism begins with that structure, with who owns the passage point and what bought it, and reads the associations as its effects. I do not think either lens is wrong. They answer different questions, and the honest course is to say which one I am using, and for what. Historical materialism says who gains from a reorganisation, who bears its costs and why it lags. Actor-network theory says how a working arrangement is assembled, what keeps it assembled and why it fails. Where complexity goes, and what keeps it understandable when it gets there, is a question of the second kind.

Most of the vocabulary that question needs is already in place. Black-boxing, mediators, generalised symmetry and translation were each introduced where the argument first called for them, and I use them here as glossed there. One more term is needed before going on. The theory's most general unit is the "actant", which in Latour's definition "can literally be anything provided it is granted to be the source of action" (Latour, 1996, p.373, quoted in Li, 2020, p.80). The corollary, as my thesis put it, is that "an object, an actant, an actor-network, can only be defined by its associations with other objects, actants, actor-networks" (Li, 2020, p.80). The word commits to nothing about the thing that acts: not whether it is a person, and not whether it means to. That is its use here. It lets a coding agent into the account without first settling whether the agent is a tool or a colleague, an argument this essay has no need to win.

Under generalised symmetry, as I described it earlier, agents are actants in exactly this sense. They open pull requests, change code, call tools and write records that other people and other agents then act on, and each of those is an effect somebody downstream has to answer to. For a practitioner the corollary does more work than the definition. If an actant is defined by its associations, there is no such thing as "the agent" apart from the model it runs on, the harness it runs in, the tools it is allowed to call and what it has been given to work from. Change any of them and you have changed what is acting, whatever the dashboard still calls it. "Is this agent good at the task?" is, on this reading, a malformed question. The answerable one is whether this assembly is good at it: this model, in this harness, with these tools, given this material. The same goes for the people. An engineer reviewing a pull request with the ticket in front of them is not the same actant as the same engineer reviewing it without.

Translation, the term I took up for lean, names how such assemblies form: a method that moves from one network into another is reassembled there, out of whatever the new network will hold. AI-assisted development is being translated into software organisations now, and the sections above have set out what it is being translated into: the theory of a program spread across a network of people and things, a complexity that every method on the list was a bet about containing, and an organisation of work that lags its tools.

At the start I stated the claim I am now obliged to earn: that generating code does not make essential complexity disappear, but moves it, into context. I also left two questions hanging: whether some complexity does in fact vanish on the way, and what stops context, once it carries the complexity, from fragmenting the way code did. Both come due here.
